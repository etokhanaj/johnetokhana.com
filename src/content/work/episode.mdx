## Overview

Episode builds landing pages from a prompt and tests them. I joined in May 2026 on contract, and <Led>I lead design and direct the front-end build for Episode's campaign management platform.</Led> I specify each change, direct Claude Code and Cursor to build it in React and TypeScript, and QA it before it ships.

My work here comes in two chapters. In the first, I set Episode's agent-first direction and designed where the agent's work lives. In the second, I designed how a business sees what's working when most of its pages have little traffic.

I work with the CEO and the CTO. They signed off my spec, and the engineers built to the requests I wrote for them. Episode is in private beta with paying customers, so every screen here uses demo data.

## What's the impact?

- Customers published 38 campaign sites in the first month of onboarding. That's a strong sign they used the product to get work out, though I wouldn't claim the design alone caused it
- In sessions, three businesses, then seven more, preferred reviewing the agent's work beside the chat to landing in the editor
- Episode became agent-first. Pages, articles, documents, onboarding, analytics questions and account campaigns all run through one agent
- ABM pages, a landing page for every company in a sales list, shipped through the agent: one sentence and one question set them up
- Charts and tests now say "not enough data yet" instead of showing a confident answer from a handful of visits

### What's the problem?

The AI did the hard part, but everything around it worked like ordinary software: a dashboard, a creation wizard, and Studio, a page editor with a panel of settings. In May I wrote the spec that set a new direction: the chat is the primary interface, and the page is something it produces.

Even with the spec, our next big feature came out in steps. ABM pages, short for account-based marketing, make one landing page for every company in a list, so a sales team can send each one a page written for it. In July I designed it the usual way: set up and map a spreadsheet, shape a first page, review the rest, publish. I directed its front end in nine parts in under two weeks, switched off for customers.

### Is this a real problem?

Before turning it on, I tried three competitors myself. Two stood out. Ploy never asked for a file. Mutiny just asks how tailored each page should be. Our design doc that day said it plainly: "we automated the decisions and manualised the plumbing."

Our flow asked people for things the agent could look up, and made them decide everything before they'd seen a page. My own feature broke the rule I'd set in May.

### What did we ship?

#### The same job, as a conversation

The same day, I redrew setup as a conversation. It cost about a week of front end, but the generation, review and publish parts carried over.

### What didn't ship

The next step was to make the theatre the main surface, with Studio's full editing inside it. I took it to a working build twice. At the merge, the CTO paused it: seven Studio behaviours had to survive untouched, and one mistake would have broken page generation, the part customers pay for. I agreed, and recommended the hybrid that shipped in September, with outputs editable in place. The full version stayed parked. It taught me to agree a stopping point with the CTO before a big build, and the team now does that before exploring.

### Editing the site from the theatre

In November we shipped the next step, still as a hybrid. A site in the theatre can be viewed or edited where it sits, beside the chat. Edit puts the page on a canvas, with its layers and pages on one side and style, goals and experiments on the other. Studio stays the full editor and is one click away, with the same section still selected.

### What's the problem?

Self-improving pages only mean something if the business can see what's working. Most of our customers weren't analysts, and most of their pages had little traffic.

In August, analytics was one long page of stacked breakdowns. Tests were worse. Episode tests versions of a page, called Takes, and Studio offered to ship any Take as the winner at any sample size, while the analytics page worked out its own answer. Two screens could disagree, and a winner could ship after a handful of visits.

### What did we ship?

#### Three tabs, one question each

In September I split analytics into three tabs. Report shows how the site is doing in one screen: the headline numbers and traffic, then the main goal and its funnel. Explore keeps every breakdown, one at a time: acquisition, pages, goals, audience, forms and experiments, filtered by 11 dimensions from campaign to country, with any row opening into a side panel. Observe is the heatmap, for clicks, scrolls, movement and rage clicks.

<Figure video="/assets/episode/analytics-tabs" alt="Analytics for Inga for Teams, a fictional site: on Report, the traffic chart with a hover tooltip, then the Team pilot started goal and its funnel from 6,396 visitors to 156 pilots; on Explore, Pages filtered to the United Kingdom and desktop, with the Start a pilot page opened in a side panel; on Observe, the home page heatmap switched between clicks, scroll depth and rage clicks" />

I also tried analytics as a tab in the theatre, beside the chat, and reverted it the next day: every future metric would have been designed twice. The chat answers with a chart card and links into the report instead.

#### Goals decide what counts

A goal is the click or page that counts as a win, like a "Book a demo" button. Tests and reports measure the main goal first. With none set, Episode suggests the ones it already tracks. "What to do next" is picked from the numbers on the server; AI only explains the "Why?" behind it.

#### Honest about thin data

A page with 40 visits will happily draw a confident chart that means nothing. So every view expects missing, thin or late data. A heatmap waits for 30 sessions and says how close it is, a funnel under 30 views says its data is limited, and a missing number shows as a dash, never a zero.

<Figure video="/assets/episode/heatmap-threshold" alt="Episode's heatmap view for Inga's Home page: Collecting sessions, 26 of 30, counts up as sessions arrive; at 30 the Low sample chip goes and the heatmap draws" />

#### One verdict per test

On 13 September I wrote the request for one verdict per test, worked out on the server, so every screen gives the same answer. The CTO built it and set the thresholds: about 100 views per Take and 90% confidence. A winning Take says by how much and how sure. When nothing separates them, the verdict says so and suggests a bigger change, which is more likely to show a gap.

<Figure src="/assets/episode/verdict-no-difference.jpg" alt="Studio's Experiments panel on an Inga for Teams page: No clear winner, 57% confidence, a result needs 90%, the Original and one Take listed, a note that a bigger change is more likely to show a difference, and Keep testing or End and keep the Original" />

#### One new Take at a time, not two

The plan allowed two new Takes to run against the original. I cut it to one: on thin traffic, a second splits the visits three ways and every verdict takes longer.

## The rules the team builds to

I wrote the rules down, and added checks so new code keeps to them.

- **Plans and checks.** I wrote the feature plans the team built from, and each cut came with a reason: account top-ups stayed manual, because automatic charges draw the same regulatory attention as a subscription. I checked every build against the design and logged 109 fixes, including a refund rule that had been reversed in the Spanish and French legal terms.

- **A design system the AI can follow.** Taking a cue from Meta's Astryx, I turned our design system into a file the AI coding tools read before they build: every colour, size and component in the product. I fixed the 152 places where the code had drifted from the design, and new drift now fails our checks, so the product stays consistent however fast we ship.
- **Trust rules for the agent.** It says when it needs you, Stop really stops a build, and failed, skipped and cancelled work each look different. Publishing, billing and anything you can't undo keep a button and a confirmation.
- **Accessibility.** Keyboard focus became visible on 117 controls, and screen readers hear a reply once it's finished, not word by word.

## Takeaways

1. People shouldn't have to tell an agent what it can find out for itself. If it can read the list, don't ask them to map the columns.
2. Run the competitors yourself. An afternoon in Ploy and Mutiny showed what our own flow was asking people to do.
3. Ship the slice that can ship. The hybrid theatre was the right call, and editing a site from the theatre followed in November as the next slice.
4. A chart from 40 visits looks as sure as one from 4,000. Design the "not enough data yet" state first, because it's what most new pages show.
