## Overview

Rivva planned the day for adults with ADHD. It began as a planner built around energy, doing hard work when you have the most of it. When our strongest sign-ups turned out to have ADHD, the product team rebuilt it for them.

I co-founded Rivva in July 2025, in a team of 12. <Led>I led design and research for Rivva's onboarding, Nia the AI planner, and the Replan and Wind Down flows</Led>, on iOS, Android, web and a browser extension. We shipped to 30+ countries within eight weeks of v1, and closed Rivva in May 2026.

The headline: after the onboarding rebuild I led, the typical new user finished their first task in 9 hours, down from 21, and activation rose from 22% to 34%. These are directional results from weekly groups of about 100 sign-ups, not a controlled test.

## What's the problem?

Most planners assume you can plan and get going on your own. For adults with ADHD, that's exactly what comes and goes. They needed help starting, and help recovering when a plan broke.

Our own numbers hid this. Activation is meant to mark the moment a new user first gets real help from an app, and ours counted people who finished setup. Users grew from 394 to 711 while it fell from 56.8% to 50.2%, and it barely predicted who stayed.

## Redefining activation

So I looked for the behaviour that did predict who came back. Within 48 hours, a person had three tasks scheduled by Nia, our AI planner, and had finished at least one. People who did this were far more likely to return. That's a correlation, not proof, but strong enough to make it our definition of activation. On it, 22% of new users activated.

I built a shared dashboard in our analytics tool (PostHog) so the team worked from the same numbers, and argued for pausing the sprint's roadmap work to fix onboarding first. We paused it and built to the new definition.

## What did research tell us?

I combined research on executive function, ADHD forums, early-user interviews and four sessions with Aanu Jide-Ojo, a clinical psychologist registered with the UK health regulator (HCPC). The same things kept coming up: a to-do list doesn't help you start, getting things out of your head helps, and once the day derails, people give up on the plan. Aanu reframed it for me: these users need interrupting more than accommodating.

## When our CTO stepped down

Until we closed, I took on engineering delivery: daily morning syncs instead of twice a week, one shared bug list we prioritised together, and a release held back until scheduling was reliable. The engineers kept the technical decisions.

## What did we ship?

### A first plan in the first session

The old onboarding was a questionnaire of about 23 steps before any help at all. I proposed and led the rebuild. We explored reordering the questionnaire, Nia asking in conversation, Nia suggesting the first task, and connecting a calendar before setup or after a first task. What shipped on 12 March 2026: fewer questions, the sensitive ones optional and private, setup moved into the app as a checklist on Home, and a first plan built from gaps in your calendar and whatever you said out loud. It still asks your age, work and working hours, so it's 13 screens, but you leave with a plan.

The paywall moved too. I'd first put it before anyone had used Nia, and same-day trial cancellations showed we were asking people to pay for relief they hadn't felt yet. It now came after Nia built your first plan, and I accepted losing people who'd have paid on the promise alone. Both changed together, so I can't split the results between them.

We also removed streaks. Adults with ADHD and autistic adults tend to be harder on themselves than their peers, and a streak that resets after one missed day turns ordinary inconsistency into visible failure.

### Nia proposes, you confirm

Nia broke big tasks into steps, replanned the day when something slipped, and turned emails into tasks. I set the rules it followed. Explicit commands act now, and anything Nia suggests asks first. Nothing saves without approval, and every change can be undone. Its voice was short and warm, with no exclamation marks and no blame.

## How did we do?

- Activation went from 22% to 34% after the rebuild
- Over the period I worked on it, sign-up to trial went from 51% to 60%, and trial to paid from 18.5% to 23.5%
- 200 paying subscribers and 53% monthly renewal, with no paid marketing

These were weekly groups of about a hundred sign-ups, not a controlled test, so I read the direction, not the size: 22% to 34% is roughly 22 people becoming 34.

## What I got wrong

I designed gentle reminders, the kind I'd want. Aanu told me to start insistent and walk it back. We shipped insistent reminders, capped at three to four a day, and I followed four people afterwards: an early lift that tailed off for those with the most persistent difficulties.

Moving setup into the app also meant fewer people set up energy, Rivva's original idea. Next time I'd have Nia ask about it after someone's first finished task.

## Why it closed

People who got a plan from Nia and acted on it were far more likely to come back. What we couldn't find was a big enough market at the price people would pay, and general AI assistants kept pushing that price down. So we closed it cleanly rather than spend other people's money proving a point.

## Takeaways

1. Measure the behaviour that predicts staying, not the easiest step to count.
2. Ask the specialist before you design the default.
3. Retention and willingness to pay are separate bets. Test what people will pay as early as you test whether they return.
